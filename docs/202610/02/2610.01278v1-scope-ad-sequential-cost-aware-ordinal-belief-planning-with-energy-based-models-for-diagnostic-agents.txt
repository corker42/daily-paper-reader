ABSTRACT 

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) diagnosis requires sequential evidence ac-quisition under heterogeneous test costs and patient burden. Fixed-modality predictors do not jointly decide which test to acquire or when the available evidence is sufficient for diagnosis. We propose SCOPE-AD (Sequential Cost-Aware Ordinal-Belief Planning with Energy-Based Models for Diagnostic Agents) for cost-aware clas-sification of cognitively normal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD cases. A mask-aware ordinal model represents un-certainty along the ordered CN–MCI–AD continuum. Retrospec-tive training records provide sampled Bellman targets for an energy-based teacher, whose action distributions are distilled into a Qwen policy. At deployment, the agent selects acquisition or diagnosis actions under availability and budget constraints without access to unacquired values. After each acquisition, the evidence and ordi-nal belief are updated before the next decision. On ADNI, SCOPE-AD achieves 77.70% Macro-F1 at an average acquisition cost of $50.46, exceeding the strongest evaluated baseline by 9.34 percent-age points. Full-modality evaluation raises Macro-F1 by only 1.89 points while increasing acquisition cost by 116.7 times. These re-sults support selective acquisition for cost-effective diagnosis. 

Index Terms — Alzheimer’s disease, sequential diagnosis, cost-aware test acquisition, ordinal learning, energy-based models 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative dis-ease, with clinical status commonly characterized as cognitively nor-mal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD along an or-dered severity continuum [1, 2]. Accurate discrimination among these clinical states is important for timely diagnosis and disease management. In practice, AD diagnosis is not necessarily a one-shot prediction problem, but often a sequential evidence-acquisition process in which decisions are made from partially observed patient information [3]. Clinicians must repeatedly determine whether the currently available evidence is sufficient for diagnosis or whether an additional test should be acquired, while candidate tests can differ substantially in monetary cost, invasiveness, and patient burden [4]. This gives rise to a central decision problem in AD diagnosis: when should the current evidence be considered sufficient, and, if it is not, which additional diagnostic modality is worth acquiring next? Existing work on sequential diagnosis spans several method-ological directions. Benchmarks such as MIMIC-CDM, MediQ, and AgentClinic evaluate interactive information seeking under incom-plete clinical evidence [5, 6, 7], whereas systems such as AMIE and MAI-DxO use self-play, dialogue fine-tuning, or multi-agent orches-tration to support iterative diagnosis [8, 9]. A second line learns test-selection policies more directly: LA-CDM and DiagAgent op-timize large language model (LLM) agents through supervised and reinforcement learning (RL), while ED-Copilot combines language-model representations with an RL controller [10, 11, 12]. Other ap-proaches replace reward-driven policy learning with explicit proba-bilistic criteria; ACTMED applies Bayesian Experimental Design to select tests with the greatest expected reduction in diagnostic uncer-tainty [13]. In parallel, non-LLM active feature acquisition methods formulate diagnostic testing as a Markov decision process (MDP) and learn cost-sensitive policies through deep, batch, or model-based RL [14, 15]. Despite these advances, most methods target differen-tial diagnosis among alternative disease hypotheses and do not ex-plicitly represent uncertainty along an ordered disease continuum. This limitation is particularly relevant to AD diagnosis. Arti-ficial intelligence (AI)-based AD studies remain dominated by bi-nary tasks such as AD–CN, MCI–CN, and AD–MCI, while multi-modal models commonly assume that all required MRI, PET, CSF, or clinical measurements are available before prediction [4, 1, 16]. Ordinal models explicitly encode label order, unlike conventional multiclass models, but encoding this order alone does not determine which modality to acquire or when to stop [2]. Conversely, recent acquisition-aware approaches, including UMoS, uncertainty-based MRI-to-PET referral, ProMUSE, and AD-specific RL policies, pro-vide only partial solutions: they focus on conversion prediction or binary classification, use restricted modality sequences, or learn ac-tions from categorical patient states [17, 18]. Consequently, an open problem is how to represent diagnostic uncertainty along the ordered CN–MCI–AD continuum from partially observed multimodal evi-dence, and how to place terminal diagnoses and acquisition trajec-tories in a unified decision space where diagnostic risk can be com-pared with the cost-adjusted value of further acquisition. To address this gap, we propose SCOPE-AD (Sequential Cost-Aware Ordinal-Belief Planning with Energy-Based Models for Diag-nostic Agents), which uses ordinal beliefs to guide modality acqui-sition and diagnostic stopping under cost and feasibility constraints. 3.2. Experiment Setup 

The ordinal belief model used two-layer 64-unit modality encoders, a 64-unit fusion layer, 0.20 dropout, temperature calibration, and five-fold patient-level cross-fitting, yielding an 87-dimensional plan-ner state. The EBM planner was a 256–256–128 Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) MLP with 0.10 dropout, trained using AdamW (learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4, and batch size 512) with planning horizon D = 7 , γ = 1 , and τE = 0 .3. The Qwen3.5-9B [22] agent used quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA) with 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization [23] ( r = 8 , α = 16 , dropout 0.05) and soft-target KL distillation with AdamW (learning rate 2 ×

10 −4 and effective batch size 16). Results are reported as the mean over three runs with seeds 42, 123, and 2026. We report class-wise recall for CN, MCI, and AD, the macro-averaged F1 score (Macro-F1), and average test cost per patient. During evaluation, only suc-cessfully acquired modalities contributed to cost.

Taken together, these controls suggest that the predominance of cognitive acquisition reflects the cost–utility structure of the benchmark rather than an inability to use other modalities: cognitive assessments provide most of the attain-able diagnostic benefit at low cost, whereas additional modalities offer comparatively limited marginal diagnostic gain relative to their acquisition cost. These findings support selective acquisition over full-modality use. The learned policy therefore tends to stop after ac-quiring cognitive evidence when its expected marginal benefit from further testing does not justify the additional cost, while retaining the ability to follow alternative acquisition trajectories when necessary. 

4. CONCLUSION 

In this work, we proposed SCOPE-AD, a cost-aware ordinal belief– energy planning framework for sequential CN–MCI–AD diagnosis from partially observed multimodal evidence. By integrating ordi-nal belief estimation, EBM-based planning, and planner-to-agent policy distillation, SCOPE-AD jointly determines whether to ac-quire evidence or make a terminal diagnosis. Experiments on the ADNI-based sequential diagnostic benchmark show that SCOPE-AD achieves 77.70% Macro-F1 at an average cost of $50.46, while full-modality acquisition provides only a 1.89-point additional gain at substantially higher cost. These results demonstrate that SCOPE-AD balances diagnostic performance and acquisition cost by avoid-ing tests with limited marginal diagnostic benefit. The ablation and cost–utility analyses further indicate that both ordinal uncertainty modeling and unified acquisition–diagnosis planning contribute to this favorable performance–cost trade-off. Overall, SCOPE-AD pro-vides an effective framework for cost-aware sequential diagnosis un-der partial multimodal evidence.
