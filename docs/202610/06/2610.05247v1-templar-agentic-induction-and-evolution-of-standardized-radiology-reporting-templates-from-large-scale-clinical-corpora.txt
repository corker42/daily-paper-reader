ABSTRACT 

Structured radiology reporting mitigates the heterogeneity of free-text reports, yet its benefits depend on high-quality reporting templates. In practice, such templates are conventionally built through labor-intensive expert consensus and therefore vary across institutions and lag behind evolving clinical practice. Large language models (LLMs) enable automated template induction, but existing approaches re-main limited: single-LLM induction is constrained by context length, and the corpus-scale method ASTAR produces a static, closed-corpus template without external grounding or downstream adaptation. To address these limitations, we propose TEMPLAR, a TEMPLate-centric Agentic framework for inducing and evolving standardized Radiology reporting templates from large-scale clinical cor-pora. TEMPLAR treats the template as a persistent central state maintained alongside two provenance-aware knowledge graphs, namely an anatomical graph that constrains template construction and a diagnostic graph that supports finding-to-diagnosis reasoning. Three agents operate on this state. The Induction Agent derives canonical clinical slots from anatomy-constrained Span-Triple atoms via dual-view similarity clustering; the Evolution Agent then assembles these slots into a hierarchical template and revises it under consistency constraints, external clinical evidence, and downstream structuring feedback; and the Clinical Agent applies the evolved template to report structuring, reconstruction, and diagnos-tic reasoning. Across four datasets (PETCT, ViMed, MERLIN, and BDMAP), TEMPLAR outperforms ASTAR, three medical LLMs, and six general-purpose LLMs in coverage, information fidelity, and diagnostic fidelity, while achieving the highest or tied-highest LLM-rated template quality. Its fidelity advantages over ASTAR persist under cross-dataset transfer, and cumulative ablations sup-port complementary contributions of its key components. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Radiology reports communicate imaging findings for clinical decisions (Ganeshan et al., 2018; Pons et al., 2016) and provide training data for medical artificial intelligence (AI) (Irvin et al., 2019; Pereira et al., 2024). Institutional conventions and individual styles produce inconsistent terminol-ogy, organization, and granularity in free-text reports (Naik et al., 2001; European Society of Radi-ology (ESR), 2018), complicating evidence extraction and normalization for clinical interpretation and AI development (Vosshenrich et al., 2023; Datta et al., 2021; Huesch et al., 2018). Structured reporting first defines a template of clinical fields and value specifications, then populates it from free-text reports (Zhang et al., 2026b;c; Fytas et al., 2024). Although LLMs increasingly au-tomate field population (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026), template construction remains largely manual, relying on institutional committees or professional-society consensus (Larson et al., 2013; D’Hondt et al., 2026). Expert involvement preserves clinical relevance, but repeated design, re-view, and adaptation require substantial labor (Busch et al., 2025), limiting deployment across tasks, institutions, and evolving practice.

LLMs offer a path toward automated template construction (Adams et al., 2023; Busch et al., 2025; Wo´ znicki et al., 2025), but context limits prevent direct processing of large reporting cor-pora (Modarressi et al., 2025). Truncation and sampling can reduce input length at the cost of incomplete coverage and uneven granularity (Zhang et al., 2026b). ASTAR scales induction by consolidating report-level concepts into canonical slots and a refined anatomical hierarchy (Zhang et al., 2026b). Its closed-corpus templates nevertheless remain static, without systematic external grounding or downstream feedback to detect and address information loss. We propose TEMPLAR, a template-centric agentic framework that induces templates from clinical corpora and maintains them as persistent states. Two provenance-aware knowledge graphs preserve clinical relations: an anatomical graph constrains construction, and a diagnostic graph supports finding-to-diagnosis reasoning (Fig. 1b). The Induction Agent extracts anatomy-constrained Span– Triple atoms, induces slots through dual-view similarity and Leiden clustering (Traag et al., 2019), and consolidates both graphs. The Evolution Agent assembles the hierarchy, revises it under con-sistency constraints and external evidence, and performs one round of feedback-guided granularity calibration. The Clinical Agent applies the template and graphs to structuring, reconstruction, and diagnostic reasoning; the Evaluation Agent assesses template coverage, template quality, informa-tion fidelity, and diagnostic fidelity. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We formulate template construction as agentic induction and evolution, treating the template as a persistent central state coupled with provenance-aware knowl-edge graphs (Fig. 1a). (2) We design an Induction Agent for anatomy-constrained slot induction, an Evolution Agent that refines the template under external evidence and downstream feedback, and a Clinical Agent that applies it to structuring and diagnostic reasoning. (3) On four datasets (PETCT, ViMed, MERLIN, and BDMAP), TEMPLAR achieves higher template coverage, information fi-delity, and diagnostic fidelity than ASTAR and nine LLMs. 22 RELATED WORK 

Structured Representations of Medical Reports. Medical report structuring converts free-text narratives into standardized, machine-readable representations, ranging from template-based clin-ical fields to normalized concepts and entity–relation graphs (Kreimeyer et al., 2017; Jain et al., 2021). In radiology, the target structure is often specified through expert-defined reporting systems. For example, PI-RADS, O-RADS, and LI-RADS standardize terminology, categorization, and re-porting for prostate MRI, ovarian and adnexal ultrasound, and liver imaging, respectively (Turkbey et al., 2019; Andreotti et al., 2020; Chernyak et al., 2018). The European Society of Paediatric Ra-diology (ESPR) Fetal Task Force has also developed structured reporting recommendations for fetal MRI (Sofia et al., 2024). With the target fields or templates specified in advance, Transformer- and LLM-based approaches primarily automate their population from free-text reports (Adams et al., 2023; Wo´ znicki et al., 2025). Beyond field-based representations, a complementary line of work explicitly captures relations among clinical concepts through entity extraction, terminology nor-malization, and relation extraction (Datta et al., 2021; Jain et al., 2021). For knowledge-graph construction from unstructured text more broadly, KGGen combines entity–relation extraction with cross-source aggregation and iterative clustering of entities and edges (Mo et al., 2025). Shifting the focus from report content extraction to template induction, ASTAR derives clinical fields and their hierarchical organization from report corpora, reducing reliance on manual template design (Zhang et al., 2026b). However, the resulting template remains a static representation derived from a closed corpus, without external knowledge grounding or adaptation to downstream structuring feedback. TEMPLAR extends this corpus-driven paradigm by incorporating external clinical evidence into template revision and using downstream structuring feedback to calibrate descriptive granularity. 

Stateful Agentic Systems. Agentic systems combine tools, specialized roles, and persistent state in interactive workflows (Xi et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024). InnoEval retrieves heterogeneous evidence for research-idea evaluation (Qiao et al., 2026); EvolveR distills interaction trajectories into strate-gies updated through subsequent feedback (Wu et al., 2026); and MedKGent assigns extraction, evidence integration, and graph updates to specialized agents (Zhang et al., 2026a). These systems motivate persistent representations revised through evidence and feedback. TEMPLAR applies this principle to reporting templates: external clinical evidence grounds semantics, structuring feedback calibrates granularity, and structural constraints and provenance support controlled updates.

Slot and Relation Consolidation. Each community is partitioned into semantic subgroups, with one slot ϕ = ( k, τ, Ω) created for each subgroup, where k denotes the canonical slot key, τ ∈{categorical , numerical , freetext } denotes its value type, and Ω is its candidate-value set. Proposals sharing a normalized key are merged while retaining their evidence. Let A(ϕ) contain the distinct identifiers of the atoms supporting ϕ. To suppress incidental concepts, we retain only slots whose distinct-atom support exceeds γs:

Φkeep = {ϕ ∈ Φ : |A(ϕ)| > γ s}, (2) where Φ is the merged slot library. Regional relation consolidation produces Ganat while preserving measurement semantics, laterality, and polarity. Separately, paired training findings and impressions yield Gdiag by normalizing diagnostic targets, including disease and clinical-status concepts, and consolidating finding–target relations with anatomical scope and provenance. Both graphs retain source evidence. Slot induction and relation consolidation are detailed separately (Appendices B.1.4 and B.1.5); representative subgraphs illustrate the resulting relations (Appendix E).

Hierarchical Assembly and Revision. From representative reports, Aevo induces a backbone H0.The first assignment round attaches slots or proposes branches; the second reassigns retained slots against the completed hierarchy. Coarse revision through within-node consolidation and cross-path concept harmonization yields T1 (Appendix B.2.1). Fine revision corrects ambiguous keys, in-compatible types, fragmented vocabularies, and numerical or unit inconsistencies. A deterministic reducer rejects edits violating structural or unit checks. Empty vocabularies are supplemented only when indexed training sentences support coherent values, producing Tf .

Cross-Source Evidence Grounding. Slots induced from the local report corpus may remain seman-tically underspecified or exhibit dataset-specific biases. Accordingly, Aevo queries PubMed, Europe PMC, and Google using each slot’s key, type, vocabulary, and anatomical path in Tf . It dedupli-cates documents by identifiers and metadata, ranks them by authority and information richness, and attaches supported evidence as slot-level reference notes. These notes clarify categorical or free-text semantics and provide units, reference intervals, or thresholds, yielding Tg (Appendix B.2.2). 

Behavior-Aware Granularity Calibration. The Clinical Agent Acli (Sec. 3.3) structures sampled training findings with Tg . For each slot s, Aevo audits its specification ϕs, extracted values Ys,aligned source contexts Cs, and a template-derived generic-value lexicon Vgen . If detailed descrip-tions repeatedly collapse to generic values (e.g., present ), the agent derives slot-specific linguistic patterns Ps. These patterns augment Tg with extraction guidance to form T ⋆, without changing Ωs.For example, mapping “a 2.1-cm spiculated lesion in the right upper lobe” to present discards its size, morphology, and location. The corresponding Ps instructs subsequent extraction to preserve these explicitly reported attributes (Appendix B.2.3). 3.4 MULTI -D IMENSIONAL EVALUATION 

The Evaluation Agent Aeval assesses template quality and downstream performance (Appendix B.3). 

Template quality is quantified by averaging the scores for design quality (13 dimensions) and clin-ical utility (6 dimensions), computed separately from LLM-as-judge (Zheng et al., 2023) and expert ratings (Appendix B.3.2). Template coverage measures how well template slots represent clinical concepts in held-out reports through LLM-based semantic matching, with coverage aggregated at the case-macro and key-micro levels (Appendix B.3.1). Information fidelity quantifies the agree-ment between reconstructed reports ˆx and the original held-out findings using ROUGE-1/2/L (Lin, 2004), chrF/chrF++ (Popovi´ c, 2015; 2017), and BERTScore (Zhang* et al., 2020), all computed directly against the references (Appendix B.3.3). Diagnostic fidelity assesses the preservation of diagnostically relevant information through LLM ratings of primary diagnosis accuracy (PDA), key finding preservation (KFP), and clinical actionability (CA), using the held-out diagnoses and find-ings as references. All three metrics are reported on a [0 , 1] scale (Appendix B.3.4).

Starting from the baseline (M1, i.e., ASTAR), we successively added the Dual-View Similarity Graph (M2), Anatomical KG Structuring (M3), Slot-Conditioned RAG (M4), Tem-plate Evolution (M5), and Diagnostic KG Reasoning (M6), with each configuration retaining all preceding components. These findings support complementary roles for concept coverage, information preservation, and diagnostic reasoning in carrying clinical evidence from free-text reports into useful structured representations. Additional ablations examine template revi-sion and evidence notes in terms of template quality and clinical utility (Appendix C.4). 95 CONCLUSION 

We presented TEMPLAR, a template-centric agentic framework for inducing and evolving stan-dardized radiology reporting templates from clinical corpora. It integrates anatomy-guided in-duction, external evidence grounding, and structuring feedback with anatomical and diagnostic knowledge graphs for report structuring and reasoning. Across four datasets, TEMPLAR achieved broader template coverage and higher reconstruction and diagnostic fidelity than ASTAR and nine LLM baselines. Its fidelity advantages over ASTAR persisted under cross-dataset transfer, while cumulative ablations supported complementary contributions from its components. By enabling scalable template construction, TEMPLAR could support clearer communication of diagnostic ev-idence for clinical decision-making and facilitate downstream clinical AI model development. This work investigates automated template construction and report processing using existing ra-diology report datasets, as described in Appendix C.1. Differences in data provenance, imaging modality, reporting practice, and language may introduce biases and limit generalization. In par-ticular, translated reports and automatically generated reports may contain artifacts that affect both template construction and evaluation. The framework is intended for research purposes. Generated templates, clinical reference notes, reconstructed reports, and diagnostic outputs may omit relevant information or contain unsupported interpretations. Automated metrics and expert assessments in this study do not establish clinical safety or readiness for deployment. Models and computing environment. Unless otherwise specified, the Induction Agent and Evolu-tion Agent use Qwen3.6-Flash for anatomy segmentation, semantic-atom induction, slot construc-tion, and template refinement. The Clinical Agent uses Gemini-3.5-Flash for template-conditioned report structuring and slot-conditioned report reconstruction. The Evaluation Agent uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash for clinical-concept extraction and semantic concept-to-slot matching. GPT-5.5 serves as the LLM judge for the automated assessment of Template Design Quality and Clinical Utility. Diag-nostic outputs were generated using Gemini-3.5-Flash and evaluated using deepseek-v4-flash. All model calls use a temperature of 0. Experiments were conducted on a server equipped with NVIDIA A800 GPUs (80GB). The GPUs were used primarily for BERTScore evaluation. 

Embeddings and multi-view graph construction. We use text-embedding-3-small to en-code clinical spans, relation triples, and training sentences. The embeddings are 1,536-dimensional, and corpus embeddings are computed in batches of 128. Within each text or relation view, row-normalized BM25 similarity and cosine embedding similarity are combined with equal weights of 0.5. BM25 uses k1 = 1 .5, b = 0 .75 , and ϵ = 0 .25 . The text and relation views are then fused with weights of 0.4 and 0.6. For each anatomical region, we construct a symmetric k-nearest-neighbor graph using k = 20 and an edge threshold of δ = 0 .25 . Leiden partitioning uses the Reichardt–Bornholdt configuration model, a resolution of γ = 1 .5, and random seed 0, and is run until convergence. Regions containing fewer than 30 atoms are retained as single communities. 

Slot induction and external evidence grounding. A slot is retained when it is supported by more than γs = 3 distinct atoms, corresponding to at least four supporting atoms. Candidate categorical values are ranked by support frequency, and at most 20 values are retained for each slot. The anatomical backbone is induced from 100 sampled training reports. For evidence grounding, the Evolution Agent queries PubMed, Europe PMC, and Google. It retrieves up to Kr = 8 records from each source, deduplicates the combined pool, and provides the six highest-ranked records for evidence-note synthesis. Retrieved documents are truncated to 6,000 characters, the evidence text supplied from each selected record is truncated to 600 characters, and each slot-level reference note is limited to 200 characters. 

Sentence retrieval and metrics. The sentence corpus is constructed from normalized and dedu-plicated training sentences. Sentences shorter than 15 characters or longer than 400 characters are discarded. For each non-empty slot-value pair, the Clinical Agent retrieves its nearest training sen-tence by cosine similarity. Retrieved sentences are deduplicated and ranked, and the top 80 sentences are supplied as linguistic and organizational references for reconstruction. ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L are reported using F-measure with stemming. chrF and chrF++ are computed at the sentence level, with word order 2 for chrF++. BERTScore uses roberta-large , layer 17, batch size 16, and a maximum sequence length of 512, and reports the F1 score.

Information fidelity measures the preservation of patient-specific information throughout the com-plete text → structure → text cycle. Specifically, given an original report x, the Clinical Agent first converts it into a hierarchical structured record z using the final template T ⋆ (Fig. 52) and then gen-erates a reconstructed report ˆx from z (Fig. 53). The reconstructed report ˆx is compared with the original report x using ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, chrF, chrF++, and BERTScore. These metrics characterize reconstruction fidelity from lexical, character-level, and contextual-semantic perspectives. The structured record z remains the sole source of patient-specific facts during recon-struction. Retrieved training sentences provide only linguistic and organizational priors and must not introduce patient-specific findings absent from z.B.3.4 DIAGNOSTIC FIDELITY 

Diagnostic fidelity is evaluated under an information-closure setting. During diagnosis generation (Fig. 54), the Clinical Agent may access only the structured representation z of the current case and the frozen diagnostic graph Gdiag constructed exclusively from the training set. The reference report and diagnosis annotation are withheld during reasoning and used only for final evaluation. The Evaluation Agent acts as an LLM judge, scoring the generated diagnostic output against the withheld reference report and diagnosis annotation. The judging prompt specifies diagnostic-output evaluation (Fig. 57). We assess three complementary criteria: PDA is rated on a 0–5 scale: 0, absent or wholly incorrect; 1, nonspecific but compatible with the reference; 2, partially correct information but an incorrect primary diagnosis; 3, correct primary diagnosis with a clinically important qualifier missing or incorrect; 4, correct diagnosis with only minor discrepancies; 5, clinically equivalent to the reference, including essential qualifiers and di-agnostic uncertainty. Important qualifiers include anatomical site, laterality, and severity or stage when specified in the reference. 23 KFP is the proportion of reference key findings correctly preserved: KFP = M/K , where K is the number of distinct diagnostically relevant reference findings and M is the number correctly retained. The reference findings and their essential qualifiers are fixed before inspecting any method’s output and shared across methods. Semantically equivalent expressions count as matches; omissions, con-tradictions, and missing essential qualifiers do not. Unsupported additions receive no credit. Cases with K = 0 are excluded from KFP aggregation, with the effective sample size reported. CA measures whether the output provides sufficient supported information for a reference-consistent clinical interpretation: 0, uninterpretable; 1, misleading; 2, usable only after major correction; 3, useful for a broad interpretation but missing important context; 4, adequate with minor omissions; 5, clear, sufficiently specific, and fully supported. Fabricated findings or unsupported recommenda-tions that could materially change clinical interpretation or management cap CA at 1. Recommen-dations are not required when the references provide no basis for them.

C.4 ABLATION ANALYSIS OF REVISION AND NOTE .To further disentangle the contributions of Revision and Note to template quality and clinical util-ity, we compare ASTAR with three sequential variants of our framework: Ours w/o (Note + Rev.) , in which both Revision and Note are removed; Ours w/o Note , which retains Revision but removes Note; and the full model, Ours , which incorporates both components. Because Note influences template usage by supplementing guidance on observation priorities, abnor-mal manifestations, interpretation criteria, and slot completion rather than by introducing additional structural fields, we focus on 13 evaluation dimensions that are closely aligned with its functional pathway. Beyond the baseline results reported in the main text, we evaluated several additional models on the same template-induction task. These baselines were not included in the main comparison because they failed during template construction or the subsequent merge stage. The observed failures arose from insufficient context capacity, unstable long-form generation, malformed structured outputs, and repetition degeneration. We report these results to document the scope of the comparison and to distinguish recoverable formatting errors from cases in which no usable template was generated. C.6.1 ADDITIONAL BASELINE MODELS 

Doubao Seed series. We first removed endpoints intended for embedding, image, video, code, or role-playing applications, as well as models with a 32k-token context window. In the screening workload, the largest induction prompt contained 26,526 tokens and required approximately 13k additional tokens for the generated template, already exceeding a 32k-token window. We screened the remaining long-context candidates using a real induction prompt (Table 6). The requests did not specify max_tokens . Nevertheless, four models terminated with finish_reason=length 

after producing between 11,541 and 21,623 output tokens, which indicates that an output-length limit was reached; the observed finish reason alone does not distinguish a service default budget from a model limit. The two Doubao 2.1 endpoints did not return within an acceptable time budget, despite responding normally to a trivial JSON request. The apparently successful seed-1.6 result was not stable under repeated evaluation. With an identical prompt and temperature set to zero, two consecutive calls produced 10,099 and 10,098 output tokens, respectively. The first completed normally, whereas the second terminated with 

finish_reason=length and five unmatched braces. In the full induction run, only 6 of 22 batches (27%) succeeded without retrying. Seventeen retry rounds were required to obtain outputs for all 22 batches, after which the merge call was still truncated at 14,514 output tokens. By com-parison, successful merge outputs from the other models contained approximately 24k–44k tokens. We therefore exclude the Doubao models from the primary comparison because they cannot reliably execute the fixed protocol, rather than because of their downstream template quality. 

Local medical models. For local models, the configured context window provides an initial screen-ing criterion. We measured peak prompt and template lengths for each dataset to characterize context demand (Table 7). The sums of the separately reported input and output peaks range from 38,204 to 43,624 tokens; these sums are screening estimates rather than measurements of a single request. HuatuoGPT-Vision-34B has a 4,096-token window without RoPE scaling and cannot accommodate even the prompt skeleton plus one report and the required output bud-get. Hulu-Med-32B has an effective 32,768-token window. Although its configuration contains 

sliding_window=131072 , sliding-window attention is disabled. The observed merge work-load also included PET/CT inputs exceeding 63k tokens and a MERLIN output of 65,067 tokens. Both models were excluded from the main comparison under the evaluated protocol. These obser-vations do not establish infeasibility for every alternative batching or hierarchical-merging schedule. The remaining long-context candidates were Lingshu-32B (128k), MedGemma-27B-IT 

(131,072), Hulu-Med-30A3 (262,144), and ClinFusion-32B (262,144). Hulu-Med-30A3

C.6.3 FALLBACK RECOVERY 

When strict JSON parsing fails, we apply a conservative fallback that retains only complete struc-tures already present in the output. Depending on the failure type, it removes an incomplete suffix, discards extra closing delimiters, or separates multiple JSON attempts produced by a generation restart. The recovered object must still satisfy the template schema, and the procedure never intro-duces new slots, keys, or values. Outputs without an extractable template remain failures. Fallback recovery prevented 12 call-level failures, all in local-model runs, while the evaluated hosted-API outputs passed strict parsing. Without this step, a malformed batch output containing a recoverable template could be discarded before merging. The fallback therefore prevents usable outputs from being discarded because of local formatting errors without changing their semantic content. C.6.4 REPETITION DEGENERATION AND CANONICALIZATION 

Structural recovery exposes a second failure mode: some models emit long sequences of repeated but individually well-formed components before truncation. In one MedGemma-27B-IT output, fallback recovered a slot whose candidate-value list Ω contained 1,719 entries but only six unique values. One 130-character value was repeated 1,714 times. Removing exact duplicates reduced this slot from 226,751 to 635 characters without deleting any unique value. Repetition was strongly concentrated in outputs that required fallback. The duplicate rate within Ω

was 5.0% for strictly parsed local outputs and 80.7% for fallback-recovered outputs. This association suggests a common generation process: repetition expands the sequence, the expanded sequence reaches an output limit, and the resulting truncation leaves an invalid JSON suffix. The association does not by itself establish that repetition caused every truncation, but it explains why malformed outputs contain substantially more duplicated content than directly parsed outputs. We observed two distinct forms of degeneration. MedGemma-27B-IT , Lingshu-32B , and 

Hulu-Med-30A3 primarily repeated values within the same Ω list. ClinFusion-32B , in con-trast, repeated entire slots. A representative ClinFusion-32B output contained 509 recovered slots but only 32 unique slots, while each individual Ω list contained two non-duplicated values. We therefore canonicalize recovered templates at two levels: exact duplicate values are removed within each Ω, and slots with the same canonical key are merged while preserving the union of their unique values. Across 114 recovered templates, value-level deduplication removed 80.7% of the recovered characters, and slot-level merging increased the total reduction to 84.2%. Template Induction – Batch Schema 

You are a radiology informatics expert building a structured slot schema for whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT reports. You will be shown $n_reports report DESCRIPTION sections (imaging findings only, not diagnoses). Induce ONE template that captures the fields these reports have in common: which anatomical regions are described, and for each, which attributes are reported and what values those attributes take. ### Output format Return a single JSON object, matching the schema of the example below exactly. Top level is "desc". Under it, each key is a region group (e.g. head_and_neck) containing organ keys, OR a flat section holding _slots_core directly. Both forms are valid; use whichever fits the content. _slots_core holds slots for attributes present in MOST reports of the batch. _slots_optional holds slots for attributes present in only SOME. Use this key only when such attributes exist. Each slot has exactly these fields: slot_id -- short unique identifier, snake_case k -- English attribute name, snake_case k_zh -- the Chinese gloss of k tau -- one of categorical, numerical, freetext omega -- a list of the values actually observed in this batch. For numerical, give value PATTERNS using <NUM> as the number placeholder (e.g. "<NUM> cm", "<NUM> x <NUM> cm", " SUVmax=<NUM>"), never concrete numbers. For categorical, list the distinct observed categories. For freetext, list representative descriptor values. ### Example (format reference only) The following is a deliberately SIMPLIFIED example. It shows the required shape, not the required coverage -- it contains only a handful of regions and slots, whereas a real batch supports many more. Follow its structure; do not copy its content, and do not stop at its level of detail. $seed_template ### Requirements Cover every anatomical region the batch actually describes, not just those in the example. The example is a scaffold, not a ceiling.

Template Merge – Schema Consolidation 

You are a radiology informatics expert consolidating slot schemas for whole-body 18F-FDG PET /CT reports. Below are $n_templates templates. Each was induced independently from a different batch of reports drawn from the same corpus, so they describe the same underlying report structure as seen through different samples. Merge them into ONE canonical template. ### Merge rules, in priority order Union of coverage. Every region, organ, and slot present in ANY input template must survive into the output. Dropping coverage is the primary failure mode of this step. When in doubt, keep it. Deduplicate by meaning, not by string. Slots describing the same attribute under different k or slot_id names are ONE slot. Merge them, keeping the clearest name. Likewise for organs and regions. Union the omega sets. When merging slots, the result’s omega is the union of the inputs’ values, deduplicated. Core vs optional by prevalence. A slot belongs in _slots_core if it appeared in _slots_core of MOST input templates; otherwise put it in _slots_optional. This distinction is the main thing merging adds over simple concatenation -- do not put everything in core. Reconcile tau conflicts. If the same attribute is numerical in some templates and freetext in others, choose the type that fits the omega values: use numerical when values are < NUM> patterns, categorical when they are a small closed set, freetext otherwise. Anatomical ordering. Order regions head-to-toe. Keep each input’s internal organ grouping where the templates agree. Fill in k_zh. Every slot in the output needs an accurate Chinese gloss, including slots merged from inputs that disagreed on wording. ### Templates $templates Output the merged JSON object alone, in the same schema as the inputs. No preamble, no commentary, no code fences.
