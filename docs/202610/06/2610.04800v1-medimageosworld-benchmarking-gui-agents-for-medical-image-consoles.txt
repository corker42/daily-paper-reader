# ABSTRACT 

Graphical consoles offer a practical interface for medical acquisition assistance, allowing agents to work through the controls and visual feedback used by human operators. Reliable assistance requires linking on-screen anatomy to acquisition decisions that determine what image evidence becomes available next. We intro-duce MedImageOSWorld, a benchmark for evaluating this capability in simulated CT, MR, and ultrasound consoles. Using screenshots and mouse-and-keyboard ac-tions, agents configure protocols, plan acquisitions, inspect the resulting images, and make corrective adjustments across seven capability levels, from console op-eration to feedback-driven control. Evaluation combines task-specific workflow checks with hidden anatomical ground truth to assess procedural completion and acquisition outcomes separately. A common evaluation protocol specifies episode conditions and interaction budgets, while recorded trajectories support analysis of how agents observe, act, and respond to acquisition feedback. Across eleven open-weight agents, success rates range from 3.0 to 25.0 on a 0–100 scale while workflow-progress rates reach 21.5–74.5: agents complete much of the console workflow but rarely acquire the intended anatomy. Success collapses between per-ception and planning, from 79–90% at the lowest three levels for the best agent to at most 12% for millimetre-level planning and 0% for closed-loop control among open-weight agents. Two proprietary agents reach success rates of about 34 and exceed the best open-weight agent mainly in acquisition quality (61 versus 42). MedImageOSWorld provides a controlled setting for studying whether general-purpose GUI agents can translate visual observations into effective medical acqui-sition decisions. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Medical image acquisition depends on operators translating an examination request into console ac-tions. They configure protocols, plan anatomical coverage or imaging planes, and assess the result-ing images. The console is the interface where an operator turns an examination goal into acquired image evidence. An inadequate acquisition may require adjustment, repetition, or escalation. The graphical console is a natural interface for acquisition assistance: it brings protocol settings, spa-tial planning, and image assessment into the operator’s existing workflow. Agents that use screen-shots and standard input actions can interact through this interface without requiring a dedicated scanner-control API or direct access to internal acquisition state. This offers a route to automation across heterogeneous console software while keeping actions and visible outcomes available for op-erator review. It also creates a demanding control problem: the agent must connect what is shown on screen to acquisition decisions whose consequences appear in subsequent images. Recent advances in general-purpose GUI agents make this interaction setting increasingly relevant. Computer-use benchmarks evaluate multimodal agents on stateful workflows over desktop applica-tions and web services (Yuan et al., 2026). Medical acquisition poses a further question: can such 1arXiv:2610.04800v1 [cs.AI] 3 Oct 2026 Preprint agents combine visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and feedback-driven control to acquire the intended anatomy without human correction during an episode? We ask not only whether an agent can operate the machine, but whether its actions produce an acquisition that meets the intended goal. The distinction arises because acquisition actions determine what evidence becomes available for subsequent decisions. We call this property action-conditioned acquisition : an agent action alters the acquisition process and thereby changes the medical-image evidence produced at subsequent steps. The agent must identify the anatomy on screen, relate image coordinates to acquisition geom-etry, and use the resulting evidence to decide whether to proceed or adjust. Changes to geometry, timing, or probe pose affect acquisition; display-only adjustments do not. An agent can complete the prescribed workflow and still acquire the wrong anatomy. Workflow completion alone does not establish that the intended anatomy was acquired. Existing environments address parts of this evaluation problem. Clinical benchmarks cover elec-tronic health records, simulated encounters, and administrative workflows (Jiang et al., 2025; Schmidgall et al., 2026; Bedi et al., 2026), while MedCUA-Bench evaluates clinical GUI interaction with safety-critical errors (Yu et al., 2026). These settings primarily concern records, software state, or predefined observations rather than imaging acquisition. ABRA, RadABench, and MedSPOT study post-acquisition viewing, radiology tool use, and interface grounding (Maksudov et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2024; Shakeel et al., 2026); they do not let the agent control the acquisition that gener-ates the underlying study. Autonomous MRI and MaRGE/MaRCoS address acquisition and scanner control (Ravi & Geethanath, 2020; Algar´ ın et al., 2024), but are MRI-specific systems rather than standardized benchmarks for general-purpose GUI agents. The remaining need is an evaluation set-ting that connects executable console workflows to action-conditioned acquisition and independent anatomical ground truth. To study this question, an environment must preserve the dependencies between planning, acquisi-tion, and assessment while retaining ground truth for the acquired result. We introduce MedIma-geOSWorld , a benchmark with three executable consoles: computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance (MR), and ultrasound. All three derive observations from XCAT-based digital human anatomy phantoms (Segars et al., 2010). The environment couples an order, a screening record, per-sistent patient and device state, a protocol library, and an action-conditioned acquisition backend. Agents select and configure protocols, plan anatomy-dependent slices, volumes, or views, acquire images, and assess their adequacy. They can re-plan, re-acquire, repeat a view, or escalate. Figure 1 separates the three stages that define an episode: visible console interaction, a hidden physical-state transition, and outcome evaluation against anatomical ground truth. MedImageOSWorld provides a setting for studying how general-purpose agents combine visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and feedback-driven control in tasks with simulated physical con-sequences. A shared capability ladder organizes these requirements and helps locate failure points. Evaluation separates workflow completion from acquisition quality using hidden ground truth in the digital subject’s coordinate frame. This makes the target of evaluation the connection between in-terface actions and measurable acquisition outcomes, rather than procedural completion alone. Our contributions are: • Action-conditioned acquisition as an evaluation setting. We formalize medical image acquisition as a GUI-agent task in which actions change the physical acquisition state and therefore the image evidence observed next. This setting separates two questions that exist-ing benchmarks conflate: whether the agent completed the console workflow, and whether the resulting acquisition captured the intended anatomy. • MedImageOSWorld, a benchmark of executable scanner consoles. Three simulated consoles (CT, MR, ultrasound) expose protocol selection, geometric planning, acquisition, review, and corrective actions through rendered screenshots and a constrained mouse-and-keyboard interface, with no access to interface or simulator state. • Ground-truth scoring in the subject’s coordinate frame. Each committed acquisition is linked to the digital subject’s coordinate frame, so a hidden oracle can grade geometry and anatomical coverage in millimetres, degrees, and coverage fractions without human assess-ment. # 3 THE MED IMAGE OSW ORLD ENVIRONMENT 

We model each episode as a partially observed interaction between an agent and a simulated con-sole. The console state has two components. The physical acquisition state determines what the scanner would acquire, such as the CT slab and isocentre, the MR imaging plane, or the ultrasound probe pose and tissue contact. The interface and workflow state records the worklist, sequence queue, workflow stage, and display settings. At each step, the agent issues a single GUI action; the simulator then updates the console state using task-relevant physical abstractions. Display-only actions, such as adjusting window width and level, modify the interface state but leave the physical acquisition state unchanged. Section 3.3 describes the separate evaluation of workflow completion and acquisition quality. Figure 1 illustrates this loop. 3.1 OBSERVATION , ACTION , AND TIME 

The primary observation is a PNG screenshot of the console rendered at a fixed viewport. The action 

space consists of constrained mouse events and an allowlisted set of keyboard events, so the agent interacts through the same visible console and standard input modalities available to a human op-erator. The agent receives no DOM, accessibility tree, simulator or interface state, script-execution primitive, hidden UI-quality text, or direct scanner-control API; task geometry and the consequences of each action are therefore available only through the rendered pixels. Before execution, a declared, version-fixed, deterministic codec may normalize model output into a legal action. For every step, an action receipt records the raw model output, any normalization or repair applied, whether the action was executed or rejected, and any additional feedback returned to the agent. Rejected actions consume steps as specified by the frozen interaction profile, and any model-specific repair logic or additional prompting is disclosed as part of the agent configuration. Simulated time advances only through executed actions, including explicit wait actions, rather than during model inference. 3.2 THREE CONSOLES 

All three consoles share a common structure (Figure 1b): planning controls modify the physical acquisition state, acquisition commits it, and the acquired images provide evidence for assessment and correction. 

Computed tomography (CT). After worklist and protocol selection, the agent plans on a scout im-age by setting the scan slab, isocentre, monitoring region of interest (ROI), and acquisition timing. The acquired stack is presented separately from the prescription, so undercoverage can be detected and corrected. Task families cover thoracic, renal, hepatic, and cardiac acquisition, including con-trast timing, repeat acquisition of clipped anatomy, and coverage under a dose-index constraint. 

Magnetic resonance (MR). The agent prescribes imaging planes and volumes on localizer images by setting plane position, orientation, field of view, and slab or slice coverage; each acquisition reslices the digital subject at the committed geometry. Within the sequence queue, acquired images guide the refinement or repetition of subsequent prescriptions for cardiac cine planes, cardiac T1/T2 mapping, and bilateral renal coverage. 

Ultrasound (US). The live renal image is rendered from the current probe position, orientation, and tissue contact, and from the depth, frequency, and focus settings. 3.3 DIGITAL SUBJECTS AND GROUND TRUTH 

Benchmark episodes use virtual subjects in simulated consoles and involve no scanning of humans or animals. The digital anatomy phantoms provide registered anatomical structures in a shared subject coordinate frame, in which every planning control and committed acquisition is expressed. CT slabs and isocentres, MR planes and volumes, and ultrasound probe planes can therefore be compared directly with hidden anatomical masks and landmarks, enabling automatic measurement of endpoint and centre error, plane position and orientation, field of view, and anatomical coverage, independent of screen coordinates or click sequences. Workflow checks use the interface state and event log to verify that required stages and actions oc-curred, whereas the hidden oracle evaluates the anatomy actually acquired; a workflow can thus complete without meeting the acquisition-quality criteria. Rendering assumptions, coordinate con-ventions, and modality-specific scope are documented in the supplementary section Simulator Ren-dering and Physical-Model Scope .

# 4 TASKS AND CAPABILITY LADDER 

Task sourcing. We first defined the capability ladder and then designed tasks against it (Figure 2a). Three medical imaging experts, assisted by a frontier AI model, proposed, cross-reviewed, and fi-nalized each task. The benchmark contains 49 task definitions: 8 for CT, 34 for MR, and 7 for ultrasound. They cover CT coverage planning, dose constraints, and undercoverage recovery; MR console operation, acquisition planning, mapping, and coverage assessment; and ultrasound kidney operation, measurement, search, optimization, and fault recovery. The tasks evaluate acquisition operations, not diagnostic accuracy. The supplementary section “Task Catalog and Reproducibil-ity Details” lists every task with a short description and its capability level; execution coverage is reported separately from task membership. 

Capability ladder. A task’s level is the capability it targets, not the interaction mechanism it uses (Figure 2a). L0–L1 concern operating the console, L2 concerns interpreting the current observation, and L3–L6 add spatial quantification, physical constraints, temporal closure, and recovery. A com-posite task is counted once, at its primary level; for example, ct_ui_smoke_routine completes the full routine workflow but is placed at L3 because it is gated on lung geometry. # 5 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Verdicts. The task scorer assigns PASS , PARTIAL , FAIL , or an undetermined outcome. Envi-ronment, receipt, or scorer faults are INVALID . Slots left unrun after a confirmed model-side early stop are counted as failures in SR. INVALID episodes and slots lost to infrastructure faults are excluded from metric denominators and reported separately. 

Workflow and acquisition are scored separately. Workflow checks cover required and forbidden events and the terminal state. Acquisition quality is measured against hidden ground truth in the digital subject’s frame: geometry in millimetres and degrees, and coverage as the fraction of the target structure inside the acquired volume. Coverage is computed from the acquired slab, never inferred from the prescribed range or a completion button (Figure 2b). Thresholds are task- and scorer-version specific; for the MR L4 2ch task, the current relaxed engineering profile gates centre, normal, field of view, and landmark errors at 8 mm, 5 degrees, 10 mm, and 5 mm. Continuous components remain visible alongside every verdict. 

Protocol freeze and aggregation. A valid comparison freezes the task version, subject or bundle, reset, observation and action profile, budgets, scorer and thresholds, runtime, and agent configura-tion. Task-side manifests are shared across compared agents; agent-side prompts, codecs, determin-istic normalizations, repair policies, and extra feedback may differ and are disclosed. Step account-ing follows the frozen profile for rejected outputs, repairs, and corrective feedback; transport-level 6Preprint retries are logged separately. Results are laid out as model × modality × level × observation con-dition, with empty modality–level cells shown as N/A. We report three 0–100 metrics. Success rate (SR) is the percentage of registered slots that satisfy the task’s complete success criterion. Progress rate (PR) is the mean percentage of registered workflow progress completed per slot. Acquisition quality (AQ) is the mean registered, normalized quality of the acquired result, computed from task-specific hidden-ground-truth measures. The legacy PARTIAL verdict provides no fractional credit to any main metric. Task-specific geometry, coverage, gate values, exhausted-budget rates, and the counts and reasons for INVALID episodes are reported alongside SR, PR, and AQ. Conditions are weighted equally within each task; the clean and defect conditions of MR renal ade-quacy each contribute half of its task score. Condition-specific results include answer frequencies, no-answer rates, and actual denominators after the exclusions defined above. Intervals resample subjects with their repeated runs grouped. The official scorer identity is the tuple of scorer code digest, oracle digest, and threshold version; changing any element creates a new scorer version.

# 6 EXPERIMENTS 

6.1 SETUP 

We ask four questions. Which registered requirements can current GUI agents satisfy across modal-ities and capability levels? How often does workflow completion coincide with acquisition quality? At which observation or corrective gate do non-passing episodes stop making progress? How sensi-tive are scores to the agent harness when the task-side protocol is held fixed? We evaluate eleven open-weight GUI and vision-language agents: Qwen3.8-27B, UI-Mate-27B, GUI-Owl-1.5-32B-Instruct, GUI-Owl-1.5-32B-Thinking, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Thinking, UI-TARS-1.5-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct, OpenCUA-32B, Gemma-4-31B, and Muse-Glimmer-30B. All are served locally with greedy decoding (temperature 0). We additionally evaluate OpenAI GPT-6 Sol ( gpt-6-sol ) and Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5 ) through their provider APIs. The target design assigns each task condition 30 subjects × 3 seeds. With clean and defect condi-tions, MR renal adequacy therefore has 180 planned slots per model. The frozen manifest records actual attempts, outcomes, exclusions, and unrun slots, together with the agent configuration for each condition. Hardware allocation and proprietary-agent harness versions are summarized in the supplementary material. Full prompts, action-codec specifications, and run configurations will be released through our Hugging Face repository. 6.2 MAIN RESULTS

In each case it restored coverage through a mechanism other than the responsible setting, so the causal-change gate failed. 6.3 WHERE AGENTS FAIL 

We decompose each scored trajectory into observation gates and corrective gates. A review gate records required viewing actions, such as opening an image and visiting specified slices or windows; it does not score interpretation. In renal tasks, causal change records a change to the responsible acquisition parameter, and an independent coverage gate measures the resulting anatomy. Other corrective gates cover geometry, parameters, and terminal state. 

Agents observe but do not correct. Observation gates are almost never the blocker. In renal MR tasks, agents review the initial series or scouts in 100% of episodes, and Claude Opus 5.5 satisfies every review gate in all 150 renal cells. Corrective gates are rarely met. Across the eleven open-weight models (about 990 valid episodes per renal task), the results are as follows: • In numeric coverage correction, agents change the responsible parameter in 14% of episodes, and only 57% of those changes restore bilateral coverage. • In coverage recovery, the corresponding rates are 7% and 36%. 8Preprint • Replacement acquisitions are completed in 25–50% of episodes, but bilateral coverage is achieved in only 6–32%. On the CT console, 1,032 of 3,084 valid episodes (33%) never committed an acquisition. Among early-stopped arms, the share of episodes reaching a committed acquisition ranged from 15% (OpenCUA-32B) to 49% (Muse-Glimmer-30B). Gemma-4-31B and Muse-Glimmer-30B exported a study in 42% and 47% of episodes and still passed none. 

Budget exhaustion and looping. Overall, 64.6% of scored open-weight episodes end with an exhausted action budget; these account for 75.7% of non-PASS episodes. By modality, the share of episodes ending this way is 70.9% in MR, 38.2% in CT, and 29.0% in ultrasound. On renal MR, exhaustion rises from 50% at L2 to 84%, 80%, and 75% at L3, L4, and L6. On CT, four agents repeated one identical click for at least 200 of 400 steps in most episodes: Qwen2.5-VL-72B (168/168), UI-TARS-1.5-7B (249/264), Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct (193/216), and OpenCUA-32B (125/228). These four agents exported in at most 2% of episodes. On ultrasound, the three zero-pass agents exhausted the 64-event budget in 89–100% of episodes, compared with 1% for Qwen3.8-27B. Whether larger budgets improve success requires a separate budget-controlled comparison. 

Primary failure reasons. Each non-PASS episode is assigned exactly one primary reason, deter-mined from its scorer gates, termination, rejected-action share (a malformed-output loop has at least 50% rejected actions), and wall time; no case was adjudicated by hand. In the reference-harness condition of the renal L6 recovery task (five agents, ten subjects, one seed, 50 episodes), the counts are: exhausting the budget without a correction: 16 (32%); declaring done without a correction: 12 (24%); a malformed-output loop: 9 (18%); reacquiring without changing the responsible setting: 4 (8%); abstaining on a correctable case: 2; never declaring done on a clean case: 2; changing the responsible setting with coverage still incomplete: 1. The remaining 4 episodes passed. Claude Opus 5.5 adds a further pattern, over-correction: it altered a correct acquisition in all 10 clean L4 and L6 cells and abstained on all 30 clean L2 cells. Agents frequently reached the required review views without making an effective correction. The supplementary material illustrates how CT and MR observations and actions can be aligned with workflow and acquisition-quality gates using schematic trajectories; these are not recorded experimental episodes. 

# 7 DISCUSSION 

MedImageOSWorld provides a controlled setting for studying how general-purpose multimodal agents apply their capabilities to specialized acquisition tasks. The interaction primitives remain screenshots, clicks, drags, and keyboard input, while successful actions depend on anatomy, acqui-sition geometry, and the current subject. An agent must combine interface operation with domain knowledge and visual reasoning to turn an acquisition goal into a working prescription. The gap between workflow progress and task success makes this combination a useful target for evaluating the transfer of general-purpose agents to specialized visual workflows. The results show where this combination currently breaks down. Success drops sharply at geometric planning, where agents must translate two-dimensional views into a position, orientation, and extent in the subject’s coordinate frame. Their acquisition choices then determine the images available for the next decision. In renal and CT recovery tasks, agents frequently reach the review stage without making an effective adjustment; some also alter an adequate acquisition in clean cases. Together, these findings point to difficulties in connecting visual evidence to the choice and verification of a corrective action. The logs do not isolate whether a failure begins with anatomical interpretation, coordinate mapping, control selection, or execution. Continuous geometry and coverage measure-ments help locate the resulting error, while task-specific gates record whether the required recovery procedure was followed. This setting also allows future evaluations to ask whether general model upgrades improve acqui-sition outcomes without additional domain demonstrations, and how much a small number of ex-pert demonstrations can help. Comparing these conditions on held-out subjects and task variations would test how readily agents adapt their existing capabilities to acquisition workflows. Improve-ments should be assessed through acquisition quality and task success alongside workflow progress, 9Preprint especially on planning and recovery tasks. Continuous outcome measures can detect useful changes before an acquisition crosses a binary success threshold. Such comparisons need fixed task and scorer versions, subjects, reset conditions, and budgets, with model revisions and harness changes recorded separately. Interpreting these comparisons requires accounting for both training exposure and evaluation condi-tions. The evaluated models’ prior exposure to related interfaces or procedures is not established, so the present results do not isolate generalization to unseen training domains. Proprietary agents also use fewer subjects and provider-specific harnesses, which limits attribution of their advantage to the underlying models. The simulated consoles measure selected anatomical and parameter objectives; transfer to scanner hardware, diagnostic image quality, and the benefit of human oversight require further evaluation. Within these limits, the benchmark makes it possible to examine whether gains in general-purpose agents translate into more effective acquisition decisions. 

# 8 CONCLUSION 

MedImageOSWorld evaluates general-purpose GUI agents on 49 medical acquisition tasks across simulated CT, MR, and ultrasound consoles. Current agents often advance through the workflow while failing to achieve the required geometry or make effective corrections. By linking interface actions to acquired images and hidden anatomical ground truth, the benchmark measures how visual understanding is translated into acquisition outcomes. It provides a controlled setting for studying whether improvements in multimodal agents carry over to specialized tasks that require spatial plan-ning and feedback-driven action.
