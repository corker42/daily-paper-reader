## ABSTRACT 

LLM agents are increasingly built for medical work and scored on clinical bench-marks. Each such score, however, comes from a model running inside an agent harness, the system that controls the loop between the model and its environment. An agent’s score is therefore a property of a model–harness pair. For medical agents, how much outcomes change with the harness has rarely been measured. Measuring this change, and explaining it, raises two challenges. First, a harness comparison must change nothing but the harness and be repeated across models and kinds of task. Second, comparing whole harnesses leaves their mechanisms bundled together, so it cannot show when an individual mechanism helps. To address these challenges, we present MedicalHarness , a controlled study of models and agent harnesses on medical tasks. We first build MedicalHarnessBench to evaluate agents on 107 tasks across four domains that each test a different harness capability. Using this benchmark, we run five open-weight models under five agent harnesses, changing only the harness within a comparison, and analyze both outcomes and execution traces. To study individual mechanisms, we build MH-Lab , a controlled harness that switches off context management, planning or tool exposure one at a time within a shared execution loop. We find that the harness and its interaction with the model account for about a quarter of the outcome variance, and that no single harness is best across models and tasks. Code and data are available at 

https://github.com/REAL-Lab-NU/MedicalHarness .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLM agents have drawn growing interest in medicine, with the promise of supporting clinical work and reducing clinicians’ administrative burden (Wang et al., 2025a; Jiang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c; 2026b). These applications require agents to act within clinical systems, from computing risk scores and finding facts in patient records to checking trial eligibility and resolving insurance denials across hospital portals. To assess how well agents complete such work, a growing set of benchmarks evaluates them in simulated clinical environments (Jiang et al., 2025; Schmidgall et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Bedi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). The resulting scores reflect both the model and the agent system that guides its interactions with the environment. Understanding these results therefore raises a basic question: how much do outcomes change when the same model runs inside a different agent system? 

The system around the model is its agent harness. Harnesses such as Claude Code (Anthropic, 2026), Codex (OpenAI, 2026), OpenClaw (OpenClaw contributors, 2026) and Hermes (Nous Research, 2026) determine what the model sees, which tools it can call, how its actions are executed, how its history is managed and when execution ends (Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c). History management affects which clinical evidence remains available (Wang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026c), while stopping rules determine whether an unfinished workflow can continue. Such differences can lead the same model to different outcomes (Zhang et al., 2026b; Yao et al., 2026). An agent’s score is therefore a property of a model–harness pair. In medicine, however, few studies compare harnesses under controlled conditions across models and kinds of task. Challenge 1: measuring and understanding harness differences. When agent comparisons change the model, serving conditions or execution budget along with the harness, the resulting score gaps mix these effects (Zhang et al., 2026b; Yao et al., 2026). A controlled comparison must therefore run the same model on the same tasks, with the serving setup and budgets held fixed. A result from one model on one task type may not hold elsewhere, so this comparison must be repeated across models and different kinds of medical task. Even under these controls, the same low score can reflect different failures, such as a wrong answer, no answer at all or an unfinished workflow, so execution traces and task diagnostics are needed to tell them apart. 

Challenge 2: understanding when individual mechanisms help. Whole-harness comparisons leave context management, planning and tool exposure bundled together, making their individual effects difficult to identify (Li et al., 2026). Whether a mechanism helps can also depend on the model and the task. Some mechanisms, such as summarization, operate only under particular conditions, so enabling them does not ensure that they are used. Studying their effects therefore requires changing one mechanism at a time within a shared execution loop, with the model, task, underlying tools and budget held fixed, and in a setting where the mechanism actually fires. To address these challenges, we present MedicalHarness , a controlled study of models and agent harnesses on medical tasks (Figure 1). We first build MedicalHarnessBench to evaluate agents across four capability domains. Using this benchmark, we compare model–harness pairs under matched execution conditions and analyze their outcomes and execution traces. To study individual mechanisms, we build MH-Lab , which switches off context management, planning or tool exposure one at a time within a shared execution loop. We find that the harness and its interaction with the model account for a substantial share of outcome variance, and that no single harness is best across models and tasks. Harnesses also usually differ more in how often they answer than in their scores on commonly answered questions, and hiding tools behind a search interface, meant to save context, can instead cost tokens or accuracy. Our contributions are: • Benchmark. We introduce MedicalHarnessBench , a benchmark of 107 executable medical tasks spanning clinical calculation, long-context retrieval, clinical data workflows and browser interaction, four domains that each test a different harness capability. • Controlled comparison study. We evaluate five open-weight models under five harnesses with three seeds ( 8,025 episodes), changing only the harness within a comparison. We pair a model– harness variance analysis with answer-production and execution diagnostics. • Controlled mechanism study. We build MH-Lab to test context management, planning and tool exposure through one-at-a-time interventions within a shared execution loop. 22 RELATED WORK 

Medical agent benchmarks. MedAgentBench evaluates task execution in a virtual EHR (Jiang et al., 2025), and AgentClinic simulates interactive clinical decision-making (Schmidgall et al., 2026). HealthAgentBench assembles executable environments across clinical data, imaging and trial matching (Liu et al., 2026a), HealthAdminBench and χ-Bench evaluate agents on healthcare administration and managed-care workflows (Bedi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), MedMCP-Calc exposes medical calculators to agents through MCP (Zhu et al., 2026), and MedMemoryBench tests memory over long patient histories (Wang et al., 2026a). These benchmarks measure what an agent can do in a medical environment. HealthAgentBench and χ-Bench also compare harnesses, but MedicalHarness is dedicated to this comparison and makes it more comprehensive and better controlled. It runs every model under every harness on more tasks, spanning four kinds that each test a different harness capability, serves all models locally under one setup so that only the harness changes, and adds controlled experiments on individual mechanisms. 

Harnesses and their mechanisms. A harness, also called a scaffold, changes what a fixed model can do (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b), yet general agent benchmarks such as AgentBench and WebArena are built mainly to compare models (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Recent work evaluates the harness itself. Harness-Bench measures harness effects across models (Yao et al., 2026), Zhang et al. (2026b) model the harness as the controller of a closed loop around the model and propose disclosure standards with a variance decomposition, Li et al. (2026) organize harness components into seven families, and Fan et al. (2026) ablate components for coding agents. Controlled comparisons outside medicine find that harness effects vary with the task and the model, from large cost but small accuracy differences on terminal coding (Vats & Golev, 2026) to accuracy gaps of up to 28 points on GAIA (Starace, 2026), and Zhang et al. (2026a) show that a score reflects the model only if the harness leaves the decisions to it. MedicalHarness also separates how often agents answer from their scores on commonly answered questions, and uses MH-Lab to switch off three mechanisms that product harnesses share, context management (Packer et al., 2023), planning and tool exposure (Qin et al., 2024), one at a time within one execution loop. 

## 3 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

We first define what a harness controls and what we hold fixed when comparing harnesses (Section 3.1). We then separate variation in benchmark scores into model, harness, interaction and repeated-run components (Section 3.2). 3.1 AGENT HARNESS 

A harness decides what the model sees, how its actions are executed, and when the episode ends. 

Definition 3.1 (Agent harness) . An agent harness is a triple of procedures h = ( Ch, U h, T h) that runs a model m on a task x. From the task’s initial state s0, while Th(st) = 0 it repeats 

ct = Ch(st), at ∼ πm(· | ct), (ot, s t+1 ) ∼ Uh(· | st, a t; x).

Here πm(· | ct) is the model’s response distribution. Ch builds the context, Uh executes the action in the task environment, collects the observation ot and updates the state, and Th decides when to stop. The state contains the history, files, plan, environment state and remaining budget. An agent is the pair (m, h ), and the task’s oracle scores its trajectory τ as Y = Rx(τ ) ∈ [0 , 1] .

Ch selects the instructions, tool descriptions and history shown to the model. State updates in Uh

include tool routing, output checks and any auxiliary model calls, such as summarization, that prepare the next turn. In our study these calls use the same model πm, are recorded in the trajectory and count toward the episode’s budget. This view guides both comparisons. When swapping harnesses, we hold the tasks and environments, model checkpoint, serving stack, context window, episode deadline and run seed set fixed. Each harness runs as released, so its prompts, tool formats, retry and stopping rules, output limits and sampling fields belong to the harness being compared. When removing a mechanism from MH-Lab ,3we change the procedures that implement it and keep the rest fixed (Section 6.4). Context management and planning change what Ch builds and Uh keeps. Tool exposure changes both the tool descriptions in Ch and the routing and feedback in Uh.3.2 MODEL –HARNESS DECOMPOSITION 

To measure how much the harness matters, we split variation in benchmark scores into four parts: the model, the harness, their interaction, and variation across repeated runs. The interaction captures whether a harness suits some models better than others. Repeated runs of the same model–harness pair can also produce different scores. We report this variation separately.

The agent must inspect records, navigate between applications and carry out the required updates, keeping track of which steps remain unfinished. 4.2 SCORING 

Outcome. Each task receives a score in [0 , 1] . We average scores over tasks within each environment. 

MedTool multiplies calculator-selection F1 by the fraction of all required outputs that are correct. The other three environments use binary outcomes. MedMemory matches normalized answers against accepted aliases. MedPlanning applies the task’s own checks, and MedWeb requires every outcome check on the final application state to pass. 

Task diagnostics (Proc.). Alongside outcome, Table 1 reports calculator-selection F1 for MedTool ,reference-set recall for MedPlanning and the share of process checks passed for MedWeb . For 

MedMemory , which has no intermediate environment checks, an LLM judge scores a condensed interaction trace reconstructed from the proxy logs (Section 6.1). Its score averages three dimensions: appropriate tool use, coherent and evidence-grounded actions, and recovery from tool failures. Section C gives the scoring rules and trace extraction procedure. 55 MH-Lab 

To study individual harness mechanisms, we build MH-Lab (MedicalHarness Lab), a minimal harness with a shared execution loop and three component switches. Implemented in LangGraph (LangChain, 2025), it follows a ReAct-style loop (Yao et al., 2023) that constructs the model’s context, calls the model, executes its tool calls, and updates the state for the next turn (Figure 3). We focus on three common mechanisms that govern how agents retain information, track unfinished work, and access tools. Medical tasks often accumulate evidence across records, require dependent steps, and involve repeated interactions with files or clinical applications. Context management determines which parts of a growing history remain available to the model. Planning maintains an explicit record of progress and remaining steps. Tool exposure determines how the model discovers and invokes available tools. Each mechanism can be varied within the same loop while preserving the task environment and underlying task tools. 

Figure 3: MH-Lab . A shared execution loop with three component switches. 

Context management. As the context fills, the same model summarizes older history for subsequent re-quests while retaining recent interactions. Summary calls count toward the episode’s budget. Switching it off retains the accumulated history without summa-rization. Planning. An update_plan tool and plan-related instructions support tracking progress. The current plan appears on every turn to make com-pleted and unfinished steps explicit. Switching it off removes the tool, instructions, reminders and displayed plan. Tool exposure. Task tools are ex-posed with their names, descriptions and argument schemas. Switching off direct exposure keeps the same tools and executors but requires discovery before calls through tool_call .For each comparison, we change one mechanism while keeping the model, task instructions, un-derlying task tools, stopping rules, and episode budget fixed. We record model inputs, tool calls, plan updates, summary events, and termination reasons to relate changes in outcomes to changes in execution. Implementation details are provided in Section H.

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced MedicalHarnessBench and used it to measure how outcomes vary with the model and harness under controlled conditions. The model explains the largest share, but the harness and its interaction with the model account for about a quarter of the variance, and no single harness is best across models and tasks. On clinical calculation and long-context retrieval, harnesses usually differ more in answer rate than in scores on questions answered by every harness. A mechanism 9changes outcomes only when it fires and when its interface fits the model. Inside MH-Lab , context management decides whether long traces survive and planning sustains longer browser workflows for 

GPT -OSS -120B . Hiding tools behind a search interface can cost tokens or accuracy when models call the tools by name. Medical agent results should therefore report the model–harness pair and, when an answer is required, its answer rate alongside the score. Section A turns these findings into design directions for medical agent harnesses.

In Proceedings of the 64th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers) , pp. 4845–4873, 2026. 12 A DESIGN IMPLICATIONS FOR MEDICAL AGENT HARNESSES 

Our execution diagnostics and mechanism experiments (Sections 6.3 and 6.4) suggest three design directions for harnesses that carry out medical tasks. 

Preserve clinical evidence. On MedMemory , accuracy falls as supporting evidence lies further back in the record. The context-management experiments also show that summaries can keep long trajectories running when history exceeds the input budget. These findings motivate context management that controls length while keeping relevant evidence accessible. A task-specific evidence record could retain key facts with their dates and source locations, allowing the agent to revisit the original record when needed. For example, after compression, a medication entry should still indicate when it was recorded and whether treatment was ongoing or had ended. 

Ground plans in clinical criteria. On trial-screening tasks, agents frequently return ineligible candidates. The planning experiments also show that maintaining a plan helps some models sustain longer workflows, but does not consistently improve outcomes. A useful direction is to make each plan step resolve a specific clinical condition. For trial eligibility, the plan could track each candidate’s inclusion and exclusion criteria, the supporting evidence and whether each condition is met, not met or unknown. Unresolved conditions would guide further retrieval before the agent decides whether to include the trial in its final result. 

Verify task completion. On MedTool , selecting the right calculators does not ensure correct values, and on MedWeb , many episodes satisfy only some outcome checks. These observations motivate checks of observable task requirements after key actions and before stopping. A simple version already helps when the model has the answer but has not filed it: enabling ZeroClaw’s submission check, which adds a reminder when the answer file is missing, raises Qwen3 .6-35B -A3B ’s MedMemory 

score from 0.42 to 0.51 (Section I). Calculator workflows could check that every requested quantity has a value and that its inputs match the patient record. Browser workflows could reread the relevant portal state after an action, such as updating an authorization request, to confirm that the intended change took effect. Such checks would identify incomplete steps and give the agent a concrete target for a retry.

20 G SPLITTING THE HARNESS VARIANCE INTO ANSWER RATE AND ANSWER SCORE 

When a task asks for an answer, an episode can end without one. For a harness h, with the model and environment fixed, let A = 1 if an episode ends with an answer, wherever the agent gives it, and Y = 0 if A = 0 . The answer rate is rh = Pr( A = 1 | h) and the mean score among answered episodes is qh = E[Y | A = 1 , h ], so that ¯yh = rhqh. With ah = rh − ¯r and bh = qh − ¯q the deviations from the means over harnesses, expanding ¯yh = ¯ r¯q + ¯ qa h + ¯ rb h + ahbh gives 

Var h ¯yh = Vr + Vq + R, Vr = ¯ q2 Var( a), Vq = ¯ r2 Var( b),R = 2¯ r¯q Cov( a, b ) + Var( ab ) + 2¯ q Cov( a, ab ) + 2¯ r Cov( b, ab ),

with moments taken over harnesses. Vr is the variance the answer rate alone would produce, and Vq

the variance the answer score alone would produce. The first term of R vanishes when answer rate and mean answer score are uncorrelated across harnesses, and the others are of third and fourth order in the deviations. The covariance is negative when harnesses that answer more score lower on what they answer, as when they answer harder items. Over the 10 model–environment pairs on MedTool and MedMemory , the first-order answer-rate share 

Vr /(Vr + Vq ) has a median of 61% and is the larger in 7 of the 10 pairs. The remainder |R| is below a third of Var h ¯yh in 6 of the 10 pairs. It is larger where the outcome variance is small (MedTool under Qwen3 .6-35B -A3B and GPT -OSS -120B ) and where answer rate and mean answer score move in opposite directions ( Nemotron -3.5-30B -A3B and GLM -4.7-Flash on MedMemory ). For 

Qwen3 .6-35B -A3B on MedTool , Vr + Vq is 3.05 times Var h ¯yh, so the negative remainder cancels about two thirds of them. Module activation. Before a mechanism inside a product harness can be ablated, it has to fire. We therefore counted how often each advertised module was used, in the archived episodes of the main study and of a pilot with Deep Agents, LangChain’s agent harness. Hermes, OpenClaw and Claude Code expose skill tools in every episode, and none of the 321 episodes per model called one. Delegation was invoked in 0–5 episodes per model, for instance in 4 of 32 MedTool episodes by Deep Agents with Qwen3 .6-35B -A3B . Hermes’s loop guards intervened in no episode, and neither Hermes nor OpenClaw forwards its reasoning-effort setting to a custom vLLM provider. Summarization fires in 2–5% of episodes at the native windows. 

Detectability. A mechanism that fires this rarely cannot be detected by switching it off. With shared sampling randomness, an episode in which it never fires ends the same way with or without it, so for a firing rate p the paired effect is at most p, and at most pp/ (1 − p) standard deviations of the paired difference. With 69 –96 paired episodes per environment, the expected z-statistic of switching summarization off is then at most 1.19 (p = 0 .02 , n = 69 ) to 2.25 (p = 0 .05 , n = 96 ), even if every firing turned a success into a failure. Our serving stack does not repeat a trajectory exactly under a fixed seed, which only makes such a mechanism harder to detect. The ablation in Section 6.4 therefore uses an input budget at which context management fires.
