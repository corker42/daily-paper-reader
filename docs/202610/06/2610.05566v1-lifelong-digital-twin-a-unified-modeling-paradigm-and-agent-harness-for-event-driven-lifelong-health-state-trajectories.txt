Abstract. Human health is a continuous, dynamic trajectory shaped by the cumulative
interplay of biological processes, clinical events, behaviors and environmental exposures
across the life course.
Unifying the full breadth of lifelong health information, including
longitudinal records, genetic variation, molecular profiles and environmental histories, is
essential for whole-person modeling and remains a major challenge. We introduce LifeLong
Digital Twin, a unified, event-driven modeling paradigm that organizes Life Events into daily
Health States and accumulates them into Lifelong Health Context. An accompanying Agent
Harness incorporates multimodal evidence beyond the language model’s textual context. We
evaluate four language models across 25 disease endpoints on three tasks: Disease Trajectory
Forecasting, Disease Risk Ranking and Multi-horizon Disease Prediction. The approach yields
marked gains over the reference condition: model-averaged F1 increases by 22.0% for disease
identification in trajectory forecasting and 18.3% for five-year disease outcomes; thyroid-
disease F1 reaches 0.669. The framework provides a foundation for whole-person digital twins
and research on personalized lifelong disease prevention.

1 Introduction
Human health unfolds through a lifetime of interacting biological processes, clinical encounters, behaviors
and environmental exposures. Their effects accumulate across years, while the evidence available to describe
them is fragmented across settings and moments in time. A diagnosis, an image or a change in physical
activity gains meaning from the history of the person behind it. Realizing the promise of digital twins for
personalized medicine [1] therefore requires a coherent, evolving account of the individual.
Existing approaches address selected aspects of personal health modeling. Delphi-2M represents diagnoses
and selected health attributes as discrete tokens, using longitudinal information for disease prediction. It is
a dedicated GPT-based disease model, rather than an application of a pretrained general-purpose language
model [2].
Work on generalist medical AI and language-model agents offers complementary directions
for multimodal interpretation and reasoning [3, 4]. Bringing these capabilities together requires a unifying
paradigm that organizes diverse personal evidence across life stages into a coherent, evolving health context.
We introduce LifeLong Digital Twin, a unified, event-driven modeling paradigm for integrating personal
health information across the life course. Its organizing principle is Lifelong Health Context: the accumu-
lated history through which observations become meaningful in relation to one another. The framework is
designed to bring evidence from clinical narratives, medical imaging, genetic information, physical-activity
trajectories and environmental exposures into this shared context. It places the individual and their evolv-
ing history at the center of modeling, connecting information across health domains and life stages. This
provides large language models (LLMs) with a common foundation for interpreting new observations and
anticipating future disease.
The paradigm begins with a unified organization of personal experience as Life Events. Events occurring
or observed on the same day form a Health State, and successive states accumulate into Lifelong Health
Context. An Agent Harness builds on this history, bringing specialized expertise to the individual’s health
questions. It relates clinical, molecular, physiological and environmental evidence to the broader personal
context, supporting reasoning across sources and over time. Together, event-driven modeling and the Agent
Harness enable an evolving digital twin in which new evidence enriches the history available for subsequent
health reasoning.
Evaluation spans 25 disease endpoints and three complementary tasks: Disease Trajectory Forecasting,
Disease Risk Ranking and Multi-horizon Disease Prediction.
Compared with the baseline setting, the
full framework delivers broad improvements in disease-event identification, diagnosis-year agreement and
prediction across short and long horizons. Particularly large identification gains for thyroid disease and
Parkinson disease illustrate the potential of the approach across distinct clinical domains. These findings
support the predictive value of Lifelong Health Context and establish a foundation for whole-person digital
twins and research on personalized lifelong disease prevention.

2 LifeLong Digital Twin
2.1 A Unified Event-Driven Modeling Paradigm
LifeLong Digital Twin organizes a person’s evolving history into individual Life Events, daily Health States,
and the accumulated Lifelong Health Context. This organization connects observations across health do-
mains and life stages, providing a shared basis for interpreting new evidence and anticipating future disease.
Figure 1 illustrates this organization through a synthetic history and a taxonomy of event categories.
2.1.1 Life Events
A Life Event is an information unit describing a personal experience, observation, or change. We write
it as ei = (τi, ci, xi), where τi is its occurrence or observation time, ci its category and type, and xi its
content. Source references, units, modality, recording time, and evidence availability accompany the event
where applicable. A family-history report, for example, is an observation made on the report date and can
also refer to an earlier period in a relative’s life.
Six categories organize the events: Background (demographics, family history, early life); Physiological
measurements (vital signs, laboratory and functional tests); Disease and clinical care (diagnoses, med-
ications, procedures); Behavior and lifestyle (diet, activity, smoking, sleep); Psychosocial (well-being,
relationships, stressors); and Environmental exposure (air pollution, noise, neighborhood conditions).
Finer event types describe the observation within each category. Categories classify the available events;
they do not specify fields that every daily state must contain.
The event category describes what the information concerns, while modality describes its form. Text, codes,
measurements, images, molecular assays, and signals can support different event categories. The source
observation, a derived measurement, and an interpretation retain their respective provenance and evidential
roles.
2.1.2 Daily Health States and Lifelong Health Context
For person p, a Health State on day d is the set of events occurring or observed on that day:
Sp(d) = {ei : person(ei) = p, τi resolves to day d},
Sp(d)̸ = ∅.
(1)
A state contains only the events of its own day. Days without events create no additional state. Thus a
family-history observation and a blood-pressure measurement can form one state, while later reports of
stopping smoking and moderate stress form another. The earlier observations remain in the history; the
later state does not restate them or infer an updated blood pressure.
For day-resolved events, the accumulated history through day t is
Cp(t) = ⟨Sp(d1), Sp(d2), . . . , Sp(dm)⟩,
d1 < d2 < · · · < dm ≤t.
(2)
This ordered component, together with events retaining coarser or unresolved time information, constitutes
Lifelong Health Context. Month-only, year-only, and interval-dated events are carried alongside the daily
states at their original precision. They are not assigned an invented day or forced into a same-day group.
Recording time is stored separately from occurrence or observation time, so a later entry can add information
about an earlier period while preserving when it was recorded.
Health State is an organization of recorded events, and Lifelong Health Context is the cumulative record
available for longitudinal reasoning. Neither requires complete observation of a person’s life. Evidence may
be sparse, missing, or conflicting, and health can change between observations. The prediction tasks and
their evaluation settings are defined in Section 3.

2.2 Direct Reasoning with Lifelong Health Context
Lifelong Health Context provides a common information basis for personal health reasoning. In the direct
Context formulation, the accumulated history is expressed as chronological event text and supplied to an
LLM together with the task. The text preserves the grouping of events into Health States and the sequence
through which the history develops (Figure 2a). The model therefore receives both earlier background and
subsequent observations when interpreting a health question.
This formulation allows a recent measurement, symptom, or lifestyle change to be considered in relation
to the individual’s prior history. The same context can support questions about current observations and
forecasts of future disease; the task specifies the requested outcome. This direct use of the accumulated
history forms the starting point for the Agent Harness described next. The task summaries in Section 3.2
illustrate the prediction questions posed against this personal history.
2.3 An Agent Harness for Personal Health
The Agent Harness extends direct reasoning with Context by connecting the same personal history to
persistent evidence and specialized health capabilities. It retains the complete admitted textual Context
as its foundation and adds three complementary components (Figure 2b): a Health State Workspace that
organizes the history and linked evidence, Specialized Skills that relate domain-specific observations to their
health meaning, and a Health Reasoning Agent that coordinates these resources around a task. Together,
they support reasoning across sources while allowing accepted evidence to enrich Lifelong Health Context
for later questions. We refer to this formulation as Harness in the evaluation.

its details while the agent retains the complete textual Context as its starting information. Chronological
and topic-based views offer complementary ways to navigate this common record.
New observations enter the history with their own time and provenance. Accepted derived evidence can
also enrich the context, with its derivation and availability recorded. Source observations, derived evidence,
and model interpretations retain distinct roles. A prediction is a task output and is not automatically
inserted as an event that has occurred. Reuse in later tasks remains subject to the applicable evidence
policy.
2.3.2 Specialized Skills
Specialized Skills connect available domain evidence to its health meaning. The framework includes five
complementary classes:
• Genetic risk: an available polygenic risk score (PRS) characterizes inherited susceptibility, interpreted
with its trait and population scope [5].
• Environmental exposure: a time-compatible air-pollution estimate supplies exposure context for the
person’s history.
• Medical imaging: findings in an imaging report describe organ phenotypes associated with the acqui-
sition.
• Omics: metabolite measurements characterize molecular profiles, with their sample timing and assay
context.
• Physiological signals: spirometry curves provide evidence about respiratory function, with recording
and quality information.
These examples describe the framework’s scope. The task and available evidence determine which Skills are
used; their run-specific configuration is a separate experimental property. Interpreting an existing imaging
report or PRS uses derived evidence and does not imply analysis of raw images or genotypes.
2.3.3 Health Reasoning Agent
The agent receives the task and complete textual Context, consults the workspace and applicable Skills,
and integrates relevant evidence into its answer. It relates specialized findings to the individual’s broader
history, connecting earlier background, recent events, and complementary measurements. The Harness thus
extends the evidence and capabilities available around the same Context foundation. Accepted evidence can
expand the record; a forecast remains a possible future outcome, separate from events that have occurred.
The reported evaluation conditions are described in Section 3.3.

3 Evaluation Design
This technical report evaluates whether a unified personal health history can support complementary forms
of disease prediction. The evaluation covers disease trajectories, risk prioritization, and cumulative out-
comes, using a common disease dictionary and three comparison conditions.
3.1 Disease Outcomes
The evaluation covers 25 disease endpoints across nine clinical categories. Each target is defined by quali-
fying ICD-10 records. The outcome is the first recorded qualifying diagnosis after the prediction cutoff for
an eligible endpoint; it does not denote biological onset or recurrence. The full definitions and exclusions
are given in Table 2 in Appendix A.
The broad all-malignant-neoplasms endpoint includes the four specified cancers: lung, colorectal, breast,
and prostate cancer. These overlapping endpoints remain distinct prediction targets. For the primary B/C
evaluation, they are combined into one cancer family, yielding 21 evaluation families. Clinical categories,
prediction endpoints, and evaluation families therefore serve different purposes.
3.2 Prediction Tasks
Task A: Disease Trajectory Forecasting.
Predict which eligible diseases will first be recorded over the
next five years and assign each predicted disease to year 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5. The output is a list of disease–year
pairs, with at most one event per disease. Evaluation distinguishes disease identification from agreement
in event timing.
Task B: Disease Risk Ranking.
Rank the five most likely eligible diseases within a specified future
window. The windows are 6 months and 1, 2, 3, 5, and 10 years. Each window has its own ranking; the list
expresses relative priority rather than asserting that every listed disease will occur. Fewer than five entries
are required when fewer than five candidates are eligible.
Task C: Multi-horizon Disease Prediction.
Predict whether each of the 25 diseases will have been
recorded by each of the same six cumulative horizons. The output is a disease-by-horizon binary forecast.
Once a disease is predicted to occur within one horizon, it remains positive at later horizons. This task
measures how well a personal history supports disease prediction at different time scales.
3.3 Models and Evaluation Settings
Using UK Biobank data [6], we compare GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Sol, and GPT-6 Astra across
Baseline, Context, and Harness. Baseline is the reference condition. Context uses the accumulated chrono-
logical history for direct reasoning, and Harness extends this formulation through the Agent Harness (Sec-
tions 2.2 and 2.3). The aggregate comparisons do not isolate the effects of individual Skills or other Harness
components.
Task B’s scored population varies by horizon because ranking evaluation requires suﬀicient follow-up and at
least one relevant outcome (Appendix B.1). All horizons are evaluated relative to a case-specific prediction
cutoff.
An unobserved outcome with insuﬀicient follow-up remains unknown and is excluded from the
relevant denominator, rather than counted as negative.
The primary measures are five-year exact-endpoint F1 for A, family-level NDCG@5 at each horizon for B,
and family-level micro-F1 for C, with five-year C performance as the primary readout. Timing agreement,
precision, recall, and other supporting measures provide complementary views of performance. Appendix B
gives the essential scoring definitions. Results are descriptive point estimates. The analysis boundaries are
discussed in Section 4.3.

4 Results
4.1 Improved Prediction across Tasks and Models
Both Context and Harness improve on Baseline in all 16 primary model–readout comparisons (Table 1;
Figure 3). Across four models, mean disease-identification F1 in A rises from 0.422 to 0.504 with Context
and 0.515 with Harness, corresponding to relative gains of 19.4% and 22.0%. Five-year C micro-F1 increases
from 0.481 to 0.559 and 0.569 (16.2% and 18.3%). Year agreement and risk ranking also improve. Harness
adds 0.30–1.45 score points over Context across these comparisons. A score point is 100 times a raw score
difference; A uses exact endpoints and primary B/C scores use disease families.

4.2 Performance across Prediction Horizons
The gains extend across six horizons, from six months to ten years (Figure 4). In B, both Context and
Harness exceed Baseline in all 24 model–horizon comparisons. In C, Harness improves in all 24, and Context
in 23; its exception is Astra at six months, with an 8.81-point decrease. Mean Harness gains over Baseline
in C range from 6.27 to 10.14 points across horizons. Task B uses horizon-specific scored populations, so
these profiles do not track a fixed cohort.
4.3 Broad Gains across Disease Outcomes
Gains are distributed across the disease spectrum (Figure 5). Using equal-weight four-model means, Context
and Harness improve F1 over Baseline for 22 and 24 of the 25 exact endpoints in A, and for 24 and 23
in five-year C. Thyroid disease and Parkinson’s disease have the largest A gains with Harness: 66.94 and
49.40 points, reaching F1 of 0.669 and 0.494 from mean Baseline F1 of zero. Effects vary by disease, model
and horizon.
Higher F1 also involves tradeoffs. For Astra at five years in C, Baseline, Context and Harness precision is
0.653, 0.653 and 0.561, respectively, while recall is 0.398, 0.531 and 0.633. The fraction of fully observed
zero-positive cases with any false positive rises from 0.647 to 0.721 and 0.868. All model averages are
descriptive, not pooled participant estimates. Paired uncertainty, disease support counts and component
ablations are unavailable; these comparisons do not establish statistical significance, effects of individual
Skills or clinical utility.

5 Future Work
5.1 Counterfactual Interventions and Personal Health Management
The event-based representation provides a basis for studying how a person’s future health might respond to
a change in behavior, environment, or treatment. An intervention can be specified relative to the observed
Lifelong Health Context, with its timing and affected factors made explicit. Estimating an intervention
effect requires a causal estimand, identification assumptions, and a validation design that addresses con-
founding, selection, and changes in measurement intensity. An altered text input and a new forecast alone
cannot establish that effect.
Future Skills could connect causal or mechanistic methods to the Health Reasoning Agent, returning
assumptions and uncertainty alongside estimates. Observed daily Health States, simulated events, and
forecasts would remain distinct, allowing personal health management scenarios to be evaluated against a
stable account of the recorded history.
5.2 Longitudinal Validation and Responsible Use
Prospective and external evaluations are needed to test how Context and Harness perform as observations
accumulate. Such studies should assess prediction quality, sensitivity to missing and conflicting evidence,
attribution to source events, and the value of individual Skills. Component ablations and matched input
conditions are needed to distinguish additional information from the effect of tool-assisted reasoning. Such
studies would clarify when additional life-course information improves prediction and how its contribution
changes across individuals and forecast horizons.
Clinical use requires evidence that improved forecasts benefit patients. Prospective studies should assess
this benefit alongside fairness, privacy, resource cost, and human oversight across populations and care
settings. Versioned evidence and temporal access controls can support these studies by preserving which
information informed each prediction. Such validation would test whether whole-person digital twins can
translate accumulated health evidence into better decisions for personalized disease prevention throughout
life.

B.1 Eligibility and Follow-up
Targets are first qualifying diagnoses recorded strictly after the prediction cutoff; records on that date
belong to prior history. An eligible endpoint is positive if observed by the horizon, negative only with
suﬀicient event-free follow-up, and otherwise unknown. Horizons use calendar months or years; follow-up
accounts for death, loss to follow-up, and record coverage.
Unknown and ineligible cells are excluded
from denominators; missing predictions are not negatives. Task B additionally requires a valid ranking,
complete follow-up, and at least one eligible candidate and positive family, yielding horizon-specific scored
populations.
